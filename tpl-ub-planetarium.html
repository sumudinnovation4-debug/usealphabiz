    {{t21|The Moon}}

    {{t22|1.3 light-seconds away}}

    {{t23|We drop low over the craters at the edge of the dark side, where shadows run for miles and the ground is older than any rock on Earth.}}

    {{t35|The last stop is as far as light allows: galaxies caught colliding, and behind them the first ones that ever shone.}}

{{t36|A ceiling}} {{t37|that}} {{t38|isn’t there}}

{{t39|The dome is twenty-three metres across and tilted fifteen degrees toward the horizon, so the sky sits in front of you as well as overhead.}}

{{t40|Two hundred and twelve seats lean back thirty degrees. Two laser projectors stitch the picture edge to edge, and the star projector in the middle of the room draws nine thousand stars as sharp points of light, never pixels.}}

{{t116|Before}} {{t117|you}} {{t118|lie back}}

{{t119|Is it suitable for young children?}}

{{t120|Small Sky is made for ages three to six and keeps the lights a little up. Every other show suits seven and over; babies in arms are welcome at Small Sky only.}}

{{t121|What if I get motion sick?}}

{{t122|Our flights are slow, but rows A to C at the back feel the least movement. Ask at the door for an aisle seat there, and look at the horizon line if you need a rest.}}

{{t123|Can I come in after the start?}}

{{t124|The doors close when the lights go down. If you are late, we move your ticket to the next show at no cost.}}

{{t125|Is the dome accessible?}}

{{t126|Step-free from the street to the seats, six wheelchair spaces, a hearing loop and a relaxed show with the lights half up on the first Sunday of every month.}}

{{t127|What happens if the roof is clouded out?}}

{{t128|The telescope evening moves indoors to a live tour of the same sky under the dome, and your roof pass stays valid for the next clear night.}}
